About

Hi, I'm Elvira Al Bataineh, a researcher working at the intersection of human–computer interaction, AI governance, cybersecurity, and international security.

My research examines how people and institutions make high-stakes decisions with AI—particularly how uncertainty, human judgment, and institutional accountability shape the use of AI-assisted security assessments. I am interested in designing decision-support systems that preserve meaningful human oversight, enable contestability, and support responsible decision-making.

As a Graduate Research Fellow at Norwich University, I am developing the AI-Informed Incident Severity Index (AIS-Index), a research framework addressing the Detection–Governance Gap between technical threat identification and coordinated institutional response. My work explores how AI-related security incidents can be classified, communicated, and escalated while maintaining human agency and accountability.

I am also a Non-Resident Fellow at the Institute for Hybrid Threats and Resilience and an International Security Fellow with the Democratic Resilience Center.

I hold three degrees from Norwich University: an M.S. in Cybersecurity with a concentration in Artificial Intelligence, an M.A. in Diplomacy with a concentration in Cyber Diplomacy, and a B.S. in Cybersecurity, summa cum laude, with a concentration in Information Warfare and Security Management. I am currently completing an M.B.A. at Norwich University with a concentration in Cyber Law and International Perspectives in Cyberspace.

I intend to pursue doctoral research in human–computer interaction, extending my work on human oversight, AI-assisted decision-making, and institutional governance.

Research

My current work centers on the AIS-Index (AI-Informed Incident Severity Index), a decision-support framework for assessing AI-enabled cyber incidents, paired with an Early Warning System for allied coordination. It addresses what I call the Detection–Governance Gap: AI can flag threats at machine speed, but the people and institutions who must decide what to do move far more slowly.

I am now bringing human–computer interaction methods into this work, to study how decision-support tools like the AIS-Index shape decision accuracy, trust calibration, and bias.
